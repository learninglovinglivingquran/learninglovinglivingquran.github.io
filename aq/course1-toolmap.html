The Arabic Toolkit

Course 1 — Foundations

This isn't a language course. Nobody here needs to speak Arabic or hold a conversation in it — that's a different project entirely. What we're building instead is a toolkit: a small, deliberate set of tools whose only job is to let you look directly at the Qur'an, instead of only ever through someone else's translation. Seven sessions. Seven tools. Take one, take all seven — each one is yours to keep either way.

Seven Sessions, Seven Tools

Click any session to open it. Each one adds exactly one new tool to the box — a reason to want it, a way to use it responsibly, and real examples straight from the Qur'an.

Science
Why This Tool

Most people believe what they believe about their faith one of two ways: because it has been reported so consistently, by so many people across so much time, that a lie could never have survived intact — or because they reasoned their way there. There's a third way of knowing, far rarer than either: direct experience. The Qur'an makes an unusual claim about itself — that its own linguistic miracle can be seen directly, by anyone equipped to look, in the Arabic itself. This session opens with a small taste of that: the word qara'a, "he read," and its connection to Qur'an itself — "a recitation" — a hint of what sits underneath a single word once translation is set aside.

Guardrail

This is not tafsīr. Learning to notice grammar isn't the same as deriving rulings — it deepens engagement with what scholars have already written, rather than replacing it.

The Tool

Every word in Arabic is exactly one of three things: ism (a noun), fiʿl (a verb), or ḥarf (a connector — "in," "to," "and"). A fiʿl always carries a sense of time built into its shape. An ism never does.

In the Qur'an
خَلَقَ الْإِنسَانَ
"He created mankind" — خَلَقَ is fiʿl, الْإِنسَانَ is ism.
فِي بَيْتِهِ
"In his house" — فِي is ḥarf, بَيْتِهِ is ism.
Science
Why This Tool

In English, "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" mean opposite things, because position carries the meaning. In Arabic, meaning is carried by an ending attached to the word itself — so word order is free to move for emphasis without ever losing track of who is doing what to whom. That freedom is part of why certain ayāt can front a word for emphasis in a way no translation can fully preserve.

Guardrail

Multiple right answers is a feature, not a failure. Classical scholars have documented cases where a single word can be legitimately read more than one way — richness, not sloppiness.

The Tool

Rafaʿ — the doer, marked with a damma (ـُ). Naṣb — the added detail, marked with a fatha (ـَ). Jarr — after a preposition, or the second word of a possessive pair, marked with a kasra (ـِ).

In the Qur'an
خَلَقَ اللَّهُ الْإِنسَانَ
اللَّهُ is rafaʿ (the doer). الْإِنسَانَ is naṣb (the detail).
بِسْمِ اللَّهِ
Both words are jarr — one after a preposition, one as the second word of a possessive pair.
اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ
Both words rafaʿ — a first glimpse of a sentence built from two nouns alone.
Mostly Science
Why This Tool

A single letter can carry theological weight. "A mercy" (رحمة) and "the Mercy" (الرحمة) are one al- apart — and a real ayah can turn on exactly that difference in a way translation has to flatten into a single choice.

Guardrail

A little Arabic doesn't make you a mufassir. Noticing a feature is a reason to go look at what scholars have already said about it — not a reason to skip past them.

The Tool

Definite vs. indefinite, and idafa — two nouns sitting side by side to mean "X of Y." The second word of an idafa is always jarr, which is exactly why last session's tool mattered.

In the Qur'an
رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ
"Lord of all the worlds" — رَبِّ + الْعَالَمِينَ, one idafa, no extra word for "of" needed.
Science
Why This Tool

One letter suffix (ه، ها، ك، ي) carries what English needs a whole separate word for. That compactness creates a density and rhythm in the Qur'an that any translation has to expand — and in expanding, flatten.

Guardrail

Isn't self-study risky without a teacher? A narrow tool, used for its actual job, is safe. The risk is in stretching a tool past what it's for — not in having the tool at all.

Guardrail

Your tools don't threaten preservation. This toolkit sits on top of 1,400 years of textual preservation and cross-checking — learning to see structure stands on that work, it doesn't call it into question.

Why This Tool

This is the payoff. A noun-first sentence (jumla ismiyya) implies something constant or permanent; a verb-first sentence (jumla fiʿliyya) implies something happening or renewing. And it's only possible because of a tool from three sessions back — word order is free to move because status markers, not position, carry the meaning.

Guardrail

Structure tells you how something is said. Full context — asbāb al-nuzūl, the wider tafsīr — tells you the fuller why and when. The tools work alongside that scholarship, not instead of it.
