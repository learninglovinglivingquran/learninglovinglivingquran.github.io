Islam in the parts that suit me

It is easy to say "me and Allah, we're good" while the worship is going well. A khutbah on Al-Baqarah raises the question of what happens when a third person enters the picture.

November 14, 20265 min read

This post collects points to reflect on from a Friday khutbah by Ustadh Nouman Ali Khan, "You Can't Surrender To Allah Selectively". The talk walks through a passage in Surah Al-Baqarah (2:204–208) that comes right after the ayat about Hajj. It describes a person who speaks beautifully about religion yet will not be corrected, and then a person who gives up their own ego to please Allah. It ends with a call to enter Islam fully, not in pieces. What follows is not a summary of the talk. It is a set of things worth sitting with, offered as personal understanding and not as tafsir, so please check them against the talk and the scholars.

The easy test and the real one

Most worship does not conflict with anything. Praying, fasting, reciting and giving rarely ask anyone to give up a grudge or an advantage. So when asked how things are between you and Allah, it is easy to answer honestly that they are fine.

The real test comes when a command of Allah touches another person: someone resented, competed with, or simply disliked. Then it is no longer a private matter between a servant and the Lord. There is a third party in the room, and feelings about them can quietly become louder than the command.

Where the passage sits

These ayat come right after the ayat of Hajj. That placement is worth pausing on. The passage may well be describing people who have just completed one of the most demanding acts of worship, not outsiders to it. Someone can travel for Hajj, stand at Arafah, and still carry one part of their faith that has quietly rotted: the part that cannot take correction, or cannot let go of a grudge. (How far the ayat are directed at pilgrims specifically is a question for the tafsir. The point here is what the placement invites a reader to consider.)

That unsettles a comfortable picture in which people are sorted into good ones and bad ones. The passage suggests something more mixed: a person can be strong in many acts of worship and still be resisting in one area. The question then shifts from "what kind of person is this?" to "which area has not yet been surrendered?", asked of others and, first, of oneself.

What Iblis actually refused

One point in the khutbah reframes Iblis. He did not refuse because he doubted that Allah exists, or that the command was real. He knew all of that. What he could not accept was what obeying would mean: bowing to Adam would concede that Adam was above him.

That makes his sin less distant than it first appears. It is a story about ego more than about disbelief, and it can be recognised wherever following something known to be right means admitting that someone else was right, or letting someone disliked come out ahead.

All of it, not the parts that suit

The word كَافَّةً (kāffatan) means "completely" or "all of it." The khutbah drew out that this can be heard two ways: all of you, and every part of life. The second is the more uncomfortable, because it closes the exits. Being devoted in the mosque but careless in how family is spoken to, or careful in worship but loose in business, is not entering completely.

Notice what the ayah warns about right after. Following Shaytan's footsteps is not one big leap. It is a series of small negotiations: this I will give, but not that.

Peace with the command, not just belief in it

The khutbah made a point about the word īmān that is worth holding on to. Its root, أ م ن, is the root of safety and security. So faith is more than agreeing that something is true. It is being settled and at ease with what Allah says, including the commands that would not have been chosen.

That suggests a better question than "is this ruling believed?" Almost always it is. The better question is: is there peace with it? Or is it accepted with the mind while argued with in the heart?

The opposite of selling out

The ayah describes giving up the self, with its ego and its need to come out ahead, in exchange for Allah's pleasure. It is striking that the verse says sells. A sale has a cost, and the cost is only felt when what is given up is something that was wanted.

The ayah also ends with Allah's kindness to His servants. Surrender is not framed as a loss. The deal comes with mercy attached.

A warning about borrowed words

The khutbah's description of the person in the earlier ayat works like a mirror. Religious language can become a tool. It is possible to say "I fear Allah" and "Allah knows my intentions" in the middle of an argument, not to seek the truth, but to win and to make the other person back off.

This is worth watching for. Reaching for Allah's name to end a conversation instead of to open one is a sign worth stopping on.

Questions to reflect on

Which command of Allah involves someone you resent, and how do you act when it comes up?
Where is Islam present in worship but missing in tone, dealings or how people are treated?
When did you last accept a correction without first defending yourself?
Is there an area where goodness elsewhere is quietly being used as cover for something that has not been surrendered?
Key takeaway Surrender to Allah is tested where a person, a grudge or a bit of pride is involved. The ayah asks for all of it, and the question to sit with is whether there is peace with what Allah has said.
