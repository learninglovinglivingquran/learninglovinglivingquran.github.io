Islam in the parts that suit me

It is easy to say "me and Allah, we're good" while the worship is going well. A khutbah on Al-Baqarah made me ask what happens when a third person enters the picture.

November 14, 20265 min read

This is a reflection on a Friday khutbah by Ustadh Nouman Ali Khan, "You Can't Surrender To Allah Selectively". He walks through a passage in Surah Al-Baqarah (2:204–208) that comes right after the ayat about Hajj. It describes a person who speaks beautifully about religion yet will not be corrected, and then a person who gives up their own ego to please Allah. It ends with a call to enter Islam fully, not in pieces. What follows is not a summary. It is what stayed with me, and it is my own understanding, so please check it against the talk and the tafsir.

The easy test and the real one

Most of my worship is not in conflict with anything. Praying, fasting, reciting, giving: none of these ask me to give up a grudge or an advantage. So when someone asks how things are between me and Allah, I can answer honestly that they are fine.

The real test is when a command of Allah touches another person, someone I resent, compete with, or simply dislike. Then it is no longer a private matter between me and my Lord. There is a third party in the room, and my feelings about them can quietly become louder than the command.

What Iblis actually refused

One point in the khutbah reframed Iblis for me. He did not refuse because he doubted that Allah exists, or that the command was real. He knew all of that. What he could not stand was what obeying would mean: bowing to Adam would concede that Adam was above him.

That makes his sin less distant than I would like. It is a story about ego more than about disbelief. I can recognise it in myself whenever I hesitate to follow something I know is right because following it means admitting that someone else was right, or letting someone I dislike come out ahead.

All of it, not the parts that suit me

The word كَافَّةً (kāffatan) means "completely" or "all of it." The khutbah drew out that this can be heard two ways: all of you, and every part of your life. I find the second one the more uncomfortable, because it closes the exits. I can't be devoted in the mosque and careless in how I talk to my family, or careful in worship and loose in business.

And notice what the ayah warns about right after. Following Shaytan's footsteps is not one big leap. It is a series of small negotiations: I will give You this, but not that.

Peace with the command, not just belief in it

The khutbah made a point about the word īmān that I want to hold on to. Its root, أ م ن, is the root of safety and security. So faith is more than agreeing that something is true. It is being settled and at ease with what Allah says, including the commands I would not have chosen.

That gives me a better question than "do I believe this ruling?" Almost always I do. The better question is: am I at peace with it? Or do I accept it with my mind while arguing with it in my heart?

The opposite of selling out

The ayah describes giving up the self, my ego and my need to come out ahead, in exchange for Allah's pleasure. It strikes me that the verse says sells. A sale has a cost, and I only feel the cost when what I am giving up is something I wanted to keep.

I also noticed the ayah ends with Allah's kindness to His servants. Surrender is not framed as a loss. The deal comes with mercy attached.

A warning about borrowed words

One part of the khutbah about the person in the earlier ayat felt like a mirror. Religious language can become a tool. It is possible to say "I fear Allah" and "Allah knows my intentions" in the middle of an argument, not to seek the truth, but to win and to make the other person back off.

I want to watch for that in myself. If I notice that I am reaching for Allah's name to end a conversation instead of to open one, that is a sign worth stopping on.

Questions I'm taking away

Which command of Allah involves someone I resent, and how do I act when it comes up?
Where am I Islamic in my worship but not in my tone, my dealings or how I treat people?
When did I last accept a correction without first defending myself?
Key takeaway Surrender to Allah is tested where a person, a grudge or a bit of pride is involved. The ayah asks for all of it, and the real question is whether I am at peace with what Allah has said.
