How a preposition makes an Arabic verb passive

You can't say "he was slept" in English, and Arabic has the same limit. But a small preposition can open a door, and you have been reciting an example of it in every prayer.

November 14, 20268 min read

In the last line of Al-Fatiha we ask to be guided away from الْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ. The first word is a passive form, "the ones who were angered at." Yet the verb behind it, غَضِبَ (ghaḍiba, "to be angry"), is the kind of verb that normally cannot go passive at all. This post is about how that works.

Two kinds of verbs

Start with a verb that does something to something. In "he brought the book," the book is the direct object. Arabic calls it the مَفْعُول بِهِ (maf‘ūl bihi). A verb that can take one is called مُتَعَدٍّ (muta‘addī, "transitive").

Now take "he slept." There is nothing the sleeping is done to, so there is no direct object. You can't say "he slept it." Such a verb is called لَازِم (lāzim, "intransitive").

Why the second kind can't go passive

A passive sentence drops the doer and promotes the thing the action was done to. "He brought the book" becomes "the book was brought." In Arabic, that promoted word is called the نَائِب الْفَاعِل (nā’ib al-fā‘il), literally "the stand-in for the doer." I'll call it the done-to.

A لَازِم verb has no direct object, so there is nothing to promote. That is why "he was slept" sounds wrong in English, and why a bare نَامَ has no passive in Arabic.

The workaround: let a preposition do the job

There is a way around this. Look at what you can say in English: "The bed was slept in." The word "in" gives the verb something to attach a done-to to. Arabic works the same way. When a verb comes with a حَرْف (a preposition) and the noun after it, that pair, called a جَارّ وَمَجْرُور (jārr wa majrūr), can step into the done-to slot. The traditional name for this is الْمُتَعَدِّي بِحَرْفٍ, "made transitive by a preposition."

Active Passive Meaning of the passive
نَامَ فِي السَّرِيرِ نِيمَ فِي السَّرِيرِ The bed was slept in.
جَلَسَتْ عَلَى الْكُرْسِيِّ جُلِسَ عَلَى الْكُرْسِيِّ The chair was sat on.
غَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ غُضِبَ عَلَيْهِمْ They were angered at (anger was directed at them).

Making one, step by step

Take the sentence جَاؤُوا بِالْكِتَابِ, "they brought the book." With بِ, the verb جَاءَ means "to bring," so the book is the thing acted on. To make it passive:

  1. Remove the doer. The doer here is hidden inside the verb (جَاؤُوا means "they came/brought"). Take the verb back to its basic هُوَ ("he") form: جَاءَ.
  2. Change the vowels to the passive pattern: جِيءَ.
  3. Leave the preposition phrase where it is: جِيءَ بِالْكِتَابِ, "the book was brought."

Notice the book is not a direct object here. It is the noun after بِ. But in the passive it plays the done-to's part, and we translate it that way.

Two rules to remember 1. The verb always stays singular masculine (the هُوَ form), even if the done-to is plural or feminine. For example, "the books were brought" is جِيءَ بِالْكُتُبِ, not a plural verb.
2. Translate the noun after the preposition as the done-to, the thing the action was done to.

How to spot it

The best method is to learn verbs together with their prepositions. جَاءَ بِ means "bring," أَحَاطَ بِ means "surround," and غَضِبَ عَلَى means "be angry at." When you meet a passive verb followed by a preposition phrase, ask which preposition belongs to that verb. Its phrase is the done-to.

This matters when there are two prepositional phrases in the sentence. You pick the one that goes with the verb.

Three examples from the Quran

Ayah Verb Preposition phrase The done-to
89:23 جِيءَ بِجَهَنَّمَ Jahannam (what was brought)
18:42 أُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ his fruit (what was surrounded)
43:71 يُطَافُ بِصِحَافٍ platters (what is passed around)

In all three, the verb is in the هُوَ form and the noun after بِ is what we translate as the done-to.

The third example has two prepositional phrases: عَلَيْهِم ("around them") and بِصِحَافٍ ("with platters"). Here the verb يُطَافُ brings the thing being passed around with بِ, so that phrase is the done-to. The other tells us who it is passed around to.

The same idea in a participle

Arabic has a passive participle too, the اسْم مَفْعُول, which is the "-ed" word: "angered," "brought," "surrounded." The two rules carry over. The participle stays singular and masculine, and the done-to attaches to the preposition after it.

Look at الْمَغْضُوب. It is singular and masculine, even though the people it describes are many. The plural lives in عَلَيْهِمْ ("on them"), which carries the done-to. That is the same pattern as غُضِبَ عَلَيْهِمْ in the table above, turned into a participle.

Does the choice of preposition add meaning? (for further study)

One more question is worth raising. If the preposition is what carries the done-to in a passive, does it matter which preposition the verb takes? Classical grammarians and commentators discussed this at length, and there are a few ideas to be aware of. These are points to think about, not settled conclusions.

1. A verb can carry the meaning of a second verb

This idea is called التَّضْمِين (taḍmīn, "implied meaning"). When a verb takes a preposition it does not usually take, some scholars say the verb is carrying the sense of another verb, the one that does take that preposition. The result is two meanings in one short phrase.

A familiar example is in the prayer: سَمِعَ اللَّهُ لِمَنْ حَمِدَهُ. The verb سَمِعَ normally takes its object directly, yet here it takes لِ. One explanation is that it carries the sense of اسْتَجَابَ ("to answer"), which takes لِ. So the meaning is "God hears, and answers, whoever praises Him."

2. A preposition may leave a trace of its own meaning

Some commentators say a preposition keeps a hint of its basic meaning even when it is used figuratively. عَلَى carries the sense of being above something, so it can suggest superiority or pressure. Examples scholars point to:

  • إِذَا اكْتَالُوا عَلَى النَّاسِ (Al-Mutaffifin 83:2). The verb اكْتَالَ is usually used with مِنْ. The use of عَلَى is read as a sign of the traders' power over the people they deal with.
  • عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا الْمُقَرَّبُونَ (Al-Mutaffifin 83:28). The verb شَرِبَ is usually used with مِنْ, but here it takes بِ.

3. Scholars disagree about how it works

The Kufan grammarians held that one preposition can stand in for another. The Basran grammarians held that it cannot, and that the verb is what carries the extra meaning, as in point 1. Some modern writers treat the two explanations as nearly the same thing. A reader should know that this is an open discussion and not a single rule.

What this might mean for passives with a preposition

In a passive, the preposition is the part of the sentence that carries the done-to, so the preposition a verb takes could matter even more. That is my own connection between the two ideas, and I have not seen it spelled out in the sources I read. It is a question to follow up on, not a claim.

Why it matters

Once you see this pattern, a whole group of Quranic phrases stops looking like irregular grammar. A singular passive verb followed by a plural noun is not a mistake. It is the preposition doing the work of a direct object. And in Al-Fatiha, it explains a word you say several times a day.

Key takeaway A verb that can't take a direct object can still go passive if a preposition steps in. The verb stays singular masculine, and the noun after the preposition is the done-to.
